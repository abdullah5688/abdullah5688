# Stop taxing stars: rebuild the prop model's priors

**The problem, in plain English.** The model treats almost every good player as if he were partly a lucky average player, then takes a little more off the top. When Brock Purdy averages about 9 yards a throw, the model assumes he will throw for about 6.8. When Amon-Ra St. Brown has a typical game of 75 receiving yards, the model expects 59. That is why most of its picks are "unders" on stars. Several separate trims add up. The model compares each player to an average player at his position instead of to players in a similar role. It forgets everything older than last season. It cuts every final projection by 2–12% ("calibration"). It builds the quarterback's yards only from his receivers, so his own skill barely counts. Separately, when a team changes quarterback or a player's role changes, the model keeps using old usage numbers for weeks, which is why 12 picks had to be removed by hand in one week. **The fix:** compare each player to players in a similar role and to his own multi-year track record, give the quarterback his own number, drop the blanket haircut, react quickly to known events such as a QB change or a trade, and give the betting market a weight that is measured rather than guessed. Every change gets tested on past weeks before we keep it.

**Bottom line (technical).** Analysts and sportsbooks don't avoid shrinking stars by shrinking less. They shrink toward a better target. The published evidence says per-play efficiency is noisy. Yards per target barely carries over between seasons (slope about 0.28, R² 0.08), and yards per carry is close to random. So heavy regression is correct. It just has to regress toward a prior built from role, usage and career history, not toward a position average. Our code already uses light pseudo-counts (45 targets for yards per target and catch rate, 70 carries for yards per carry). That means pure efficiency shrinkage can't be the whole story behind a 21–24% shortfall. Reading the code, the likelier causes stack on top of each other:

- a flat multiplicative calibration scale (0.88 on receptions, 0.92 on receiving yards);
- a single prior season at half weight;
- QB passing yards that inherit every receiver's shrinkage while the QB's own yards per attempt cancels out of the calculation;
- a probable mismatch between the games used for receiver efficiency and the QB adjustment.

The usage problem is a regime-change problem, not a decay-rate problem: a 4-game half-life is already fast. The recommended redesign is a role-conditional, multi-season empirical-Bayes prior; a QB-anchored team passing total; probability-level calibration fitted by player tier; event-triggered usage resets; and a market blend whose weight is fitted out of sample. Each step is gated by a walk-forward test that checks for bias by player tier, not just overall accuracy.

**About the evidence.** Most of the outside sources behind this report were read only as search-result summaries, because the sites were blocked for full reading (Footballguys, 4for4, SumerSports, PFF, Establish The Run, Unabated, Action Network, Medium and others). Treat every quoted figure as "reported, needs verification" until someone reads the original. The diagnosis of our own model comes from reading `README_MODEL.md`, `engine/model.py` and `engine/calibration.json`. It is inference from code, not from re-running the model, and step 0 of the plan exists to confirm it.

## Elite efficiency is noisy, so the target of shrinkage matters more than its strength

The research is consistent that **volume is sticky and per-play efficiency is not**, and good projection systems are built around that split.

| Stat | Reported stability | What it implies |
|---|---|---|
| QB yards per attempt | About 396 attempts before it is half skill, half luck ([Footballguys/Harstad](https://www.footballguys.com/article/HarstadRegression02)). Net yards per attempt has a year-to-year slope of about 0.5, R² 0.24 ([Football Perspective](http://www.footballperspective.com/tag/regression/)) | A full starter season keeps about half its edge over average |
| Yards per carry | 1,978 carries to stabilize, "basically a random number generator" ([Footballguys](https://www.footballguys.com/article/HarstadRegression02)) | Nearly full shrinkage toward team or offensive-line context |
| Yards per target | Next season's Y/T = 5.84 + 0.28 × this season's, R² 0.08 ([Football Perspective](https://www.footballperspective.com/yards-per-route-run-yards-per-target-and-targets-per-route-run/)) | Heavy shrinkage, but only toward a role-specific mean |
| Target share | Year-over-year r about 0.55 ([4for4](https://www.4for4.com/2023/preseason/most-predictable-wide-receiver-stats)) | Light shrinkage |
| Targets per route run | "Very sticky" ([Football Perspective](https://www.footballperspective.com/yards-per-route-run-yards-per-target-and-targets-per-route-run/)). Stabilizes in about 7 games, from a study about 12 years old ([Intentional Rounding](https://intentionalrounding.com/when-do-receptions-and-touchdowns-per-route-run-stabilize/)) | Light shrinkage |
| Yards per route run vs. expected yards per route run | 0.51 vs. **0.67** ([SumerSports](https://sumersports.com/the-zone/revisiting-yards-per-route-run/)) | Role-based expectation is stickier than raw production |

All of these are from search summaries and come from different samples, so the absolute values can't be compared directly. The ranking, though, is consistent across sources.

The table seems to argue for *more* shrinkage of receiver efficiency, not less. A 0.28 year-to-year slope implies about 2.5 seasons' worth of phantom average targets, against our 45. That is an inference: the year-over-year slope also captures QB and role turnover, so it overstates the shrinkage needed within a season. The way out of this apparent contradiction is the key finding of the whole report. **Yards per target mostly measures how a receiver is used, not how good he is.** Harstad found yards per reception correlated 0.70 with Y/T across 180 receivers, and 28 of 31 outliers were over-performers relative to a usage-based expected Y/T ([Footballguys](https://www.footballguys.com/article/HarstadRegression03)). SumerSports' finding that *expected* yards per route run beats raw yards per route run (0.67 vs. 0.51) says the same thing: a receiver's role persists ([SumerSports](https://sumersports.com/the-zone/revisiting-yards-per-route-run/)). Shrinking St. Brown toward "all WRs" pulls him toward a mix of deep threats, slot receivers and fourth receivers. Shrinking him toward "high-target-share slot receivers with his career record" barely moves him, because that prior already sits near his true level.

Baseball worked this out decades ago. Marcel weights the last three seasons 5/4/3 and adds a fixed dose of league average, 1,200 plate appearances, once to the pooled history ([Tango archives](https://www.tangotiger.net/archives/stud0214.shtml)). That gives about 50% regression for a player with about 300 weighted plate appearances and only about 14% for one with about 1,800 ([Tango archives](https://tangotiger.net/archives/stud0346.shtml)). Tango also fits each player's own context baseline before regressing ([Tango archives](https://www.tangotiger.net/archives/stud0214.shtml)). The general empirical-Bayes method is the same: the shrinkage weight is κ/(κ+n), with κ fitted from the data, and separately by population, because pooling different roles distorts the prior ([r-statistics.co](https://r-statistics.co/Empirical-Bayes-in-R.html); [metricgate](https://metricgate.com/docs/empirical-bayes/)). One open-source fantasy project's own backtest found that heavy regression "over-regress[ed] established players and under-weight[ed] recent form". It cut its prior to about 2 games' worth ([tefirman/fantasyfb PR #160](https://github.com/tefirman/fantasyfb/pull/160)). That is a single unaudited project, but it is the closest published match to our symptom.

A straightforward check falls out of this (inference). Linear shrinkage toward a mean must land *between* the player's observed rate and that mean. League yards per attempt is about 7. Projecting a 9.0-YPA quarterback at 6.8 means something other than shrinkage is pulling him down.

## Our code stacks four separate trims on every star

Reading `engine/model.py` and `engine/calibration.json` points to compounding causes rather than one bad parameter. All of the following is code-reading inference, to be confirmed by the step-0 diagnostic.

**The calibration scale is a flat tax.** The model multiplies every simulated outcome by a per-market scale. The current values are **0.88 for receptions, 0.92 for receiving yards, 0.90 for rushing yards, 0.92 for rushing attempts, 0.96 for pass attempts and 0.98 for passing yards**. A flat scale takes the most yards from the biggest projections: 8% of a 75-yard receiver is 6 yards, while 8% of a 20-yard receiver is under 2. The scale was fitted on all players pooled, most of whom are low-volume. So it is probably correcting an over-projection of fringe players by taxing stars. The research notes call this the prime suspect for tier-dependent bias. Tier-specific calibration is an established idea; there is even a patent on calibrating projections by performance tier ([USPTO 9082074](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/9082074), patent text not read). For St. Brown, the 0.92 scale alone explains roughly a quarter of the 75→59 gap. For Purdy, the passing-yards scale (0.98) explains very little, so his gap has to come from elsewhere.

**The quarterback's own skill barely enters his passing yards.** QB yards equal the sum of his receivers' simulated yards. Each receiver's yards per target is shrunk toward a position mean, and the leftover "other" target bucket is valued at the plain WR average. The quarterback's own yards per attempt enters only as a ratio, `qb_factor = QB's YPA / mean YPA of the QBs in the usage sample`. When the 3-start gate is on, the usage sample is the quarterback's own starts, so the ratio is about 1.0 and his 9.0 YPA effectively drops out. Every receiver's shrinkage then passes through to the QB, giving what the notes call an "average QB throwing to average receivers" projection. Industry practice runs the other way. Mike Clay projects team by team, starting with team dropbacks and team passing efficiency and allocating shares down ([ESPN](https://africa.espn.com/fantasy/football/insider/story/_/id/33621386/mike-clay-learned-doing-2022-fantasy-football-projections), search summary). DraftKings' play-by-play simulator samples each completion once, so passer and receiver yards come from the same draw ([DraftKings Engineering](https://medium.com/draftkings-engineering/modeling-football-combining-ml-models-and-monte-carlo-simulations-170fd58cbe11), search summary).

**The efficiency and QB adjustments look at different games.** Receiver efficiency is computed from *all* current-season games plus half of last season, whoever was throwing. The QB adjustment is computed relative to the quarterbacks in the *usage* sample only. If a receiver's efficiency history includes games with a backup or a former quarterback, while the gate restricts usage to the current starter's games, then the QB factor is about 1.0 and the backup-era efficiency is never corrected. Whether this hit Purdy depends on whether his receivers' 2025–26 history includes non-Purdy starts. Step 0 checks this directly.

**History is too short, and the comparison group is wrong.** Only one prior season counts, at half weight, and the prior is the pooled position mean. That mean is volume-weighted (total yards over total targets), which partly avoids the "backups drag the average down" problem. It is still a single number for every WR, TE or RB, whatever their role. Veterans like Purdy and St. Brown lose the multi-season record that separates elite players from flukes. Rushing has the opposite problem: 70 phantom carries is far lighter than the roughly 2,000-carry stabilization point. That is consistent with the README's known issue that **rushing-yard overs hit 5–7 points less often than predicted**: high-YPC backs keep too much of their lucky efficiency.

Smaller contributors also stack up. These include defensive factors shrunk with only 5 games of pseudo-weight, the 0.8–1.15 clip on the QB factor, and the gap between median and mean in skewed yardage. The order of the factors in a multiplicative chain doesn't change the total, but the number of factors does: five 5% trims compound to about 23%. Roughly that size of shortfall shows up in both named cases.

## Usage goes stale because the model has no notion of a regime change

A 4-game half-life is already aggressive. The fantasyfb backtest settled on roughly a 7-week half-life ([GitHub](https://github.com/tefirman/fantasyfb/pull/160)), and PFF reports that raw target share explains only 41% of next week's target share ([PFF](https://www.pff.com/news/nfl-refining-coach-i-was-open-a-model-comparing-actual-vs-predicted-target-shares), search summary). Making the decay faster would add noise without fixing the 12 excluded picks. What fails is the handling of *discrete events*. With fewer than 3 starts by the new quarterback, the model falls back to all games (the old regime) and flags the pick. At 3 starts it flips abruptly to the new QB's games only. Vacated shares are spread in proportion to existing shares. New arrivals have no ramp-up.

The evidence on these events is mostly descriptive, but it is consistent. Team pass volume "belongs to the team and the scoreboard," while efficiency follows the quarterback: expected pass rate has year-to-year r of about 0.45 for QBs who stay on the same team ([Patton Analytics](https://pattonanalytics365.substack.com/p/putting-yourself-in-a-hole)). A QB's personal target-distribution profile takes about 600 attempts to stabilize ([RotoWire](https://www.rotowire.com/football/article/the-stats-room-are-qb-targets-predictable-41122)). The 3-start gate therefore throws away good team-level information early and over-trusts a noisy QB-specific split later. Backups tilt toward running backs, for example 23% of Tyson Bagent's attempts against 15% of Caleb Williams' ([FantasyPoints](https://www.fantasypoints.com/nfl/articles/2025/qb-positional-target-tendencies)); this isn't controlled for game state. Vacated targets don't flow proportionally. They go largely to newcomers and high draft picks ([Fantasy Footballers](https://www.thefantasyfootballers.com/articles/vacated-targets-predicting-the-future-in-fantasy-football/)), often to the same position ([PFF](https://www.pff.com/news/fantasy-target-distribution-trends)), and sometimes to running backs ([PlayerProfiler](https://www.playerprofiler.com/article/vacated-targets-why-they-matter-and-whos-eating/)). Traded receivers ramp up over 1–3 weeks: Tank Dell went from 13% of routes in his debut to 55% the next week ([FantasyPoints](https://www.fantasypoints.com/nfl/articles/2026/week-10-fantasy-football-trade-targets-buy-low-sell-high), search summary). Players listed questionable lose about 8–10% of production ([4for4](https://www.4for4.com/how-injury-affects-nfl-fantasy)).

No public source describes a Kalman-filter or changepoint model for NFL player usage. Professional shops appear to rely on human news overrides ([ETR](https://establishtherun.com/fantasy-point-projections/)), which is what our analyst currently does by hand. The closest formal model is Glickman and Stern's state-space model of NFL team strength, which lets the underlying level drift faster when personnel changes ([Glickman & Stern](https://glicko.net/research/nfl.pdf)). The practical version for us (inference) has two layers. A slow per-player usage level stays shrunk toward a role prior. An event trigger fires on a QB change, trade, teammate injury or return, or a snap-share jump of about 15 points over two games, and when it fires, recent games get much more weight. The 15-point threshold is not sourced and should be tuned in the backtest. The analyst already writes these events into `context.json`, so the trigger can read them directly. Splitting targets into routes × targets per route run would let the coaching decision (routes) jump while the player trait (targets per route) stays shrunk. Our data has no route counts, but snap share is already loaded and can stand in until we add a route source.

## The market is a useful prior, but our blend is guessed, single-book and never checked against the close

Some lean toward unders is legitimate. Weekly NFL prop unders reportedly hit about **55% in 2017–2019 but about 50% in 2020** ([PFF](https://pff.com/news/bet-nfl-betting-2020-week-11-thursday-night-football-player-props)). Passing-yard unders were the only profitable blind category from 2020 to mid-2022 ([PFF](https://pff.com/news/nfl-betting-2022-midseason-player-prop-performance-review)). Rufus Peabody says "the public bets overs," and books nudge star lines up late; Christian McCaffrey's receiving line went from 32.5 to 35.5 on over money ([The Volume](https://www.iheart.com/podcast/1119-the-volume-78406522/episode/straight-fire-super-bowl-prop-148378416/); [The Ringer](https://www.theringer.com/2022/9/28/23375387/gamblers-super-bowl-episode-3-rufus-peabody)). There is counter-evidence too: one analysis of 1,170 optimizer-picked legs found unders and overs at an identical 54.8% ([Upside](https://upside.tools/research/unders-vs-overs-player-props)). The documented effect is a few points of hit rate, and the 4.5–7% vig consumes much of it. A model whose picks are *mostly* unders, concentrated on stars, is far outside that range. The decisive test (inference) is whether the model also leans *over* on low-usage players. "Stars under, scrubs over" is the signature of shrinkage toward a population mean, not of market inefficiency.

Sharp practice treats a devigged consensus as the prior and regresses the model toward it. Unabated builds this into its tools to "regress [your] own projections toward the market to avoid model bias" ([Unabated](https://unabated.com/articles/introducing-market-based-prop-projections)). A paper titled "Beating the market with a bad predictive model" argues that a weak model combined well with market odds can still add value ([arXiv 2010.12508](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.12508), title-level only). Three details matter for us. First, no prop book is a true sharp originator: Pinnacle reportedly outsources props and limits them to about $250 ([sportsbooks.ag](https://sportsbooks.ag/reddit/best-prop-betting-site-reddit/), anecdotal). Books also copy each other, and main lines across 8 US books typically differ by only 1 receiving yard or 3 passing yards ([Steezanomics](https://steezanomics.com/learn/do-all-sportsbooks-have-the-same-prop-line/)). So we should use a multi-book consensus, which we already fetch from FanDuel, DraftKings and BetMGM, rather than FanDuel alone. Second, devig method barely matters at near-even prices; methods differ by under 1 point ([Bet Hero](https://betherosports.com/blog/devigging-methods-explained)). Third, closing line value on props is real but noisy, because low limits mean little money sets the close ([SharkBetting](https://www.sharkbetting.com/blog/closing-line-value-clv-explained); [football-data.co.uk](https://football-data.co.uk/blog/pinnacle_limit.php)). Even so, it is the only short-sample check of edge we have, and `ledger.py snapshot` already exists but is never scheduled.

The fixed 50/50 blend hides some of the bias but also halves any real edge. The better approach (inference) is to fit the weight on log-odds, p = σ(w·logit(p_model) + (1−w)·logit(p_market)), by market and by tier, on held-out props. Practitioners commonly start the model's weight at 0.1–0.3 until it proves itself (unsourced practitioner prior). Backtesting against the market needs historical lines, which The Odds API sells for player props from **May 3, 2023**, in 5-minute snapshots, one event per request, on paid plans ([The Odds API](https://the-odds-api.com/historical-odds-data/)). That would let our existing ~9,000-result backtest be scored against real lines instead of only against outcomes.

## Conclusion

Our model doesn't believe too little in regression; it regresses toward the wrong place and then adds extra trims on top. The literature says efficiency deserves *heavier* shrinkage than our 45-target pseudo-count, but toward a prior that already knows a player's role and career. Done that way, stars stay near their true level while fringe players get pulled down. That also explains why a single flat calibration scale can never fix the problem: the error runs in opposite directions for different tiers. The same logic applies to usage and the market. A fixed half-life, a fixed 3-start gate and a fixed 50/50 blend are all rules that should be learned from data and triggered by events. The owner's best protection is a single test that runs on every change: **group past projections by player tier and check that the average miss is close to zero in every group.** If the model passes it and still leans under on stars, that lean is probably edge.

## Implementation plan, in priority order

Every step runs through the existing `backtest.py` walk-forward (2025 weeks 4–18 plus 2026 weeks 4–10, about 9,000 player-market results), with each parameter fitted only on data before the week being scored. As in the README, fit on 2025 and score on 2026. Every step reports the same **tier-bias scorecard**:

- the mean of (actual − projected median), split by prior-season efficiency decile and by projection decile;
- the slope from regressing actual on projected, where about 1.0 is good and above 1.0 means over-shrunk;
- Brier score and reliability at half-point lines, by market;
- pinball loss or CRPS for the whole distribution;
- the over/under pick share split by tier.

Keep a change only if overall Brier and CRPS don't get worse **and** the top-decile bias shrinks, with bootstrap confidence intervals showing the gain isn't noise.
